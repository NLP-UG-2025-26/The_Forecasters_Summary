## Weronika Sawicka
The chapter begins by discussing the concept of meaning and shows that meaning is not simple or fixed. Words can have dictionary meanings, personal or emotional associations, and meanings that depend on context. For example, the same sentence may express sincerity or sarcasm depending on how it is used.
The chapter then introduces the morpheme, which is the smallest unit of meaning in language. Some morphemes can stand alone as words, such as book or happy, while others must be attached to another word, such as un- or -ed. These are called free and bound morphemes.
The text also explains the difference between inflectional and derivational morphemes. Inflectional morphemes change grammatical features without creating a new word, such as adding -s for plurals or -ed for past tense. Derivational morphemes create new words or change the grammatical category of a word, such as happy becoming unhappy or teach becoming teacher.
Another important idea in these pages is word formation. English creates many new words through processes such as adding prefixes and suffixes or combining words into compounds like blackboard. These processes help expand the vocabulary of the language.
Towards the end of the section, the chapter introduces lexical semantics, which studies relationships between word meanings. Words can be related through similarity, opposition, or shared categories. The chapter emphasizes that meaning is part of a larger system and that understanding language requires studying how words relate to one another and how context shapes interpretation.

## Bartosz Żurek
The chapter continues by examining how linguists and lexicographers study the meanings of words. It begins with an overview of different types of dictionaries, including monolingual, bilingual, abridged, unabridged, specialized dictionaries, and thesauruses. The text explains that dictionaries are designed for different audiences and purposes, but all aim to provide clear information about word meanings.
The chapter then describes how lexicographers determine the meanings of words. A major example is the Oxford English Dictionary (OED), which was created using millions of citation slips collected from written texts. These citation slips recorded words in context and helped lexicographers trace how meanings developed over time. The discussion highlights the importance of studying words in authentic contexts rather than in isolation.
The text also explains how modern dictionary making has changed with the use of large electronic corpora. Instead of relying solely on handwritten citation slips, lexicographers can now analyse billions of words using computer software. Concordance tools allow researchers to observe patterns of usage, identify collocations, and discover multiple meanings of words more efficiently.
Another important topic is polysemy, the idea that a single word can have several related meanings. The chapter uses examples such as the word *chair*, which can refer both to a piece of furniture and to a leadership position. This demonstrates that word meanings are often flexible and influenced by context.
The chapter then discusses how dictionary definitions are created. Lexicographers aim to produce definitions that are accurate, concise, and easy for users to understand. Modern dictionaries often arrange meanings according to frequency and include examples from real language use to show how words function in context.
Towards the end of the section, the chapter introduces componential analysis, a method of describing word meanings through semantic features. Words such as *man*, *woman*, *boy*, and *girl* can be analysed using features like human, adult, and male. However, the chapter notes that this approach has limitations, particularly when dealing with abstract concepts such as freedom, happiness, or fear. As a result, linguists have developed alternative approaches that focus on relationships between words and broader patterns of meaning.

## Magda Makowska
The chapter goes on to explore two major aspects of the English lexicon: semantic relations between words and processes of word formation. It first examines how words are related through meaning and then discusses the mechanisms by which new vocabulary is created and introduced into the language.
Three important semantic relations are presented: synonymy, antonymy, and hyponymy. Synonymy refers to words with the same or very similar meanings. Although words such as help and assist may be interchangeable in certain contexts, the chapter emphasizes that absolute synonymy is extremely rare. Most so-called synonyms are actually near-synonyms that differ in connotation, formality, or usage. For example, hard and difficult cannot be substituted freely in every context, while buy and purchase differ mainly in their level of formality.
Antonymy describes relationships between words with opposite meanings. Many antonyms are gradable, meaning they represent opposite ends of a scale, such as old/new or hot/cold. Others are binary opposites, such as dead/alive, where no intermediate state exists. Antonymic relationships occur not only among adjectives but also among nouns, verbs, and adverbs.
Hyponymy is a hierarchical relationship in which the meaning of a more specific word is included within that of a more general one. Thus, poodle is a hyponym of dog, while dog is a hypernym of poodle. Such relationships create semantic hierarchies and help organize vocabulary into categories. The discussion of hyponymy also introduces prototype theory, according to which some category members are perceived as more typical or central than others.
One important process mentioned in the word formation subsection is meaning extension, whereby an existing word acquires a new sense. Examples include the use of memory in computing and the development of 9/11 from a calendar date into a label for a historical event. Meaning extension often involves metaphorical usage, as seen in expressions such as the head of an organization or the heart of the problem.
Among the most productive methods of creating new vocabulary is compounding, which combines two existing words or morphemes into a new lexical item, such as firefighter or spacewalk. Affixation, through the addition of prefixes and suffixes, also remains a major source of new words. Another important source of vocabulary growth is borrowing, as English has historically adopted numerous words from languages including Old Norse, German, Arabic, Chinese, and Spanish.
The chapter further discusses functional shift (conversion), in which a word changes grammatical category without changing form. Examples include nouns becoming verbs, as in to bottle or to google. Other processes include clipping, which shortens existing words (flu, phone), and blending, which combines parts of two words (brunch, smog, podcast).
New words may also arise through acronyms and abbreviations. Acronyms, such as NATO or laser, are pronounced as words, whereas abbreviations, such as CIA or DVD, are pronounced letter by letter. The chapter also examines back formation, where a new word is created by removing a perceived affix (e.g., edit from editor), and folk etymology, in which speakers reinterpret unfamiliar forms according to familiar linguistic patterns, as in the development of pea from pease.
Overall, the chapter demonstrates that lexical meaning is organized through systematic semantic relations, while the English vocabulary continues to expand through a variety of productive word-formation processes. These mechanisms illustrate both the structure of the lexicon and its dynamic capacity for change.
## Dominik Folga
The chapter concludes by examining the concept of deixis, which refers to the "pointing" function of language. Words can have invariant dictionary meanings but point to completely different external referents depending on the context of the utterance. The text identifies three primary types of deixis: referential, spatial, and temporal.
Referential deixis governs how words refer to specific entities and is split into two categories: exophora and endophora. Exophoric reference points outside of a text to the situational context, such as first- and second-person pronouns ($I$, $we$, $you$) that identify the immediate participants in a conversation. Because it is highly context-dependent, exophora predominates in spoken language. In contrast, endophoric reference points to other words within the text itself to create cohesive ties, making it the dominant form in written language. Endophora can be anaphoric (pointing back to an introduced noun phrase) or cataphoric (pointing forward to an anticipated noun phrase). The text also notes that pronouns can have broad reference when they refer to an entire sequence of speech or an abstract idea rather than a single noun. Furthermore, referents can be generic (all members of a class) or specific (actual entities), with specific referents being either indefinite or definite.Spatial deixis (or space deixis) situates the speaker or writer in space. It relies on demonstratives like this and that, spatial adverbs like here and there, and certain prepositions. These terms are interpreted based on distance from the speaker, using proximal forms for things close to the speaker and distal forms for things farther away. The chapter notes that the choice between these terms is not always strictly dictated by physical distance, as demonstratives can also point abstractly to preceding statements or situational features.
